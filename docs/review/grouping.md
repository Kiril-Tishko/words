# Stories tab: how the words are grouped

The outline is in `data/stories/outline.json`: 20 parts, 200 chapters, 2,152 anchor words.
Every one of the 2,212 words and 72 exclamations has exactly one home.
The other 132 words are not anchors, but each one is named in its chapter's scene, and in no other scene, so the other team can place them without guessing.

## Principles I used

- **Course order.** First survival (meeting people, café, shopping, transport, hotel, phone), then daily life, then the wider world. Specialized topics come last.
- **Frequency goes from common to rare.** The median chapter is 27 for Top 1000 words, 59 for Top 2000, 80 for Top 5000 and 119 for Top 20K.
- **Easy for Ukrainians beats rare in English.** Only 434 of the words are Top 1000–5000, so early scenes also use Top 20K words that are everyday in Kyiv: latte, avocado, smartphone.
- **Real scenes.** Every chapter is one place and one small event, told with one cast: Anna (a student in Kyiv), her friend Max, Anna's mama, dad, brother Taras and grandparents, Max's sister Sofia, and friends Lisa and Ira.
- **Words that go together stay together:** taxi, GPS, highway, terminal, then passport, baggage, duty-free, then hotel, check-in, Wi-Fi.
- **Near-synonyms are split up.** For example: hotel / motel / hostel, cop / policeman / constable, champion / champ, update / upgrade, lava / magma, marijuana / cannabis / ganja.
- **Look-alike pairs are in different parts** (pairs that share one Ukrainian form): cash / cache, comma / coma, disc / disk, block / bloc, lama / llama, gin / jinn, madam / madame, cream / creme.
- **Abstract words live in scenes**, not in a list. Idea, plan and chance are in Max's weekend plan; feedback and insight are in his first presentation; dilemma and conflict are in a problem at work.
- **Anchor order is story order.** Writers can follow the anchors from first to last.
- **Levels:** A1 (24 chapters), then A2 (93), then B1 (83). A1 stories have 8–13 words; B1 stories have up to 16.

## Exclamations and "other stuff"

- 67 of the 72 exclamations are in the last part, in 9 small chapters grouped by function.
- Only in this part are similar words put together on purpose (ha, ha-ha, ha-ha-ha). For these words, the function is the lesson.
- Five social formulas are anchors in the first story chapters instead: hello, welcome, sorry, OK, goodbye. They are the words a learner needs on day one, and a "meeting people" scene is made of them. Their casual twins (hey, yo, bye, okey-dokey, pardon) stay in the Exclamations part.
- "Other stuff" from the main list that works like a shout went to the Exclamations part: LOL, game over, SOS.
- Forms of address are used in story scenes, not in the Exclamations part: sir, Miss and Mrs. in Kyiv; madame and monsieur in Paris; Herr and Frau in Berlin; senpai in Tokyo; amigo in Mexico.
- Greek letters are a scene: Anna reading street signs in Athens.

## Please decide

1. **The five social formulas.** Keep hello, welcome, sorry, OK and goodbye in the first chapters (my advice), or move them to the Exclamations part.
2. **Sensitive scenes.** These are written for adults, are factual, and come late: sex education (c081), addiction (c082), suicide and self-harm hotlines (c078), coming out (c055), war (c161), gangs and drugs (c164), true crime (c165), terror (c166), the Holocaust (c186). Should the tab hide them by default, like the rude cards?
3. **City scenes** open part 16, next to politics, because they are about how a city is run. They can move to before Nature if you prefer.
4. **Specialist medicine** (surgery, cancer, the brain) is in Science and technology, not in Health, so that Health stays A2.
5. **A few contrasts are kept on purpose** inside one scene, where the scene is about the difference: madame / mademoiselle, communism / socialism, parliament / senate.
6. **The tab has 200 chapters**, the top of the planned range. To make it shorter, the easiest change is to merge some small A1/A2 chapters.
7. **Hard but clear topics.** Some chapters cover areas that are narrow but easy to understand, such as Greek letters, dog breeds and currencies. Writers should keep these stories light.
